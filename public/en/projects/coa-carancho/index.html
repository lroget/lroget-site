The idea

A club that did not stop at watching birds.

COA Carancho, the Palermo Bird-Watching Club (Club de Observadores de Aves de Palermo), is part of the COA network of Aves Argentinas, a hundred-year-old NGO. I founded it in 2011 as COA Palermo, coordinated it from 2013 to 2015 and took part until 2020.

The idea was to put taking part before watching, with birds as the starting point for doing many other things.

The place

Parque Tres de FebreroLakesEcoparqueBotanical GardenPlanetariumRacecourseRosedalParque Olímpico

Our base was Parque Tres de Febrero, the Palermo woods: the lakes, the Ecoparque, the Carlos Thays Botanical Garden, the Galileo Galilei Planetarium, the racecourse, the Rosedal rose garden. We also ran activities in the Costanera Norte and Costanera Sur reserves, and trips to other places.

What we did

012015

Seasonal censuses

I designed them with Diego Carús, who later took over from me as coordinator. The idea was that anyone could take part, whatever their experience: fixed routes and groups that mixed experts and beginners.

Together we added several species that had never been recorded in Palermo before. The censuses became a tradition and are still held.

022012–2016

Native plantings

One of the first things we did beyond watching birds was to improve the place where we held our activities: bringing back the original landscape with native plants.

We organised several plantings in Parque Olímpico (Av. Dorrego and Av. Figueroa Alcorta), with the operations office of Parque Tres de Febrero and the Native Trees Group of Aves Argentinas. We planted more than 100 trees and shrubs; some are huge today. Informally, the square is known as "Parque Carancho".

100trees and shrubs as of 22 May 2016. More came later. Each dot is one.

032014–2016

Raptor releases

Next to the park is the Ecoparque (the former Buenos Aires Zoo), home to the Raptor Conservation and Rehabilitation Programme (PCRAR) and today's Fundación Caburé-í. That let us release rehabilitated birds in the park and hold demonstrations.

They were the COA's biggest events: the release on 10 January 2015 freed ten birds in front of more than 200 people. The release of Rafael, a southern caracara and the 500th bird to go through the programme, mattered so much that the club stopped being COA Palermo and became COA Carancho.

042013–2018

Talks and courses

Open talks at the Planetarium, the Botanical Garden and Aves Argentinas, and a short ornithology course. At the Planetarium I gave "¿Qué nos dice el cielo sobre la tierra?" (What does the sky tell us about the earth?) with Diego Carús in 2016, and in 2018 we presented "Mirar lo invisible" (Seeing the invisible) with Diego Luis Hernández.

Also: pond-sampling outings (charqueadas) with El Acuarista, and the "Yo no uso pirotecnia" (I don't use fireworks) campaign in 2015.

05May 2016

#caranchofest

By its fifth anniversary the COA was probably at its peak. First as a joke and then for real, we set out to hold a month-long celebration: #caranchofest.

May brought six activities, all organised as a team.

  6. Sat28Autumn census and closing

062016

Costanera Norte

At the Aves Argentinas centenary celebration in the Buenos Aires City Legislature, several of us who were worried about the Costanera Norte Ecological Reserve met. At the time it was in a legal vacuum and almost abandoned.

A week later we organised a walk with the dean of the School of Exact and Natural Sciences of the University of Buenos Aires (UBA), people from the City Government, Aves Argentinas, Fundación Vida Silvestre Argentina and FARN, and birdwatchers and students. That was the start of Amigos de la Reserva Ecológica Costanera Norte, a group that worked to untangle the knots between the City Government and the UBA.

What lasted most were two clean-up days, organised horizontally: dozens of people pulled seven tonnes of rubbish out of the wetland, in two trailers provided by CEAMSE, the state waste-management company of the metropolitan area.

Where you live

A COA near you?

COAs are part of the Aves Argentinas network. This is what worked for us in Palermo.

  1. A visionUntil then, my sense was that COAs failed to draw young people: they were small groups of veteran birders. They taught me a lot, but deep down they bored me. The idea was to build a group I would want to be part of.
  2. A purposeActivities open to everyone to discover the natural surroundings. None of them charged a fee, and all the team's work was volunteer.
  3. A teamA multidisciplinary group pulling in the same direction. That was the real strength.
  4. An identityWe were the first COA with its own logo, and the identity was kept in every publication. Communication was key.
  5. An opportunityThere was nothing like it in Palermo. The neighbourhood has plenty of green space, sits between the city's two ecological reserves, opens onto the Río de la Plata and is home to science, conservation and outreach institutions, which we kept in close contact with.
  6. A methodOne meeting a month to plan and share out roles. That is how we pulled off big activities.
